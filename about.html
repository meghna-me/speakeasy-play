2, 4, 6

In 1960 Peter Wason gave people those three numbers and asked them to work out the rule behind them. They could test as many sequences as they liked. He answered only yes or no.

Almost everyone tested sequences that agreed with the rule they already suspected. 8, 10, 12 — yes. 20, 22, 24 — yes. Then they declared, and they were wrong. The rule was any three increasing numbers. Most people never found it, because almost nobody tried a sequence they expected him to reject.

Why the end screen counts surprises

A round you are already sure about tells you nothing. And a round that would genuinely separate two theories you still hold is close to a coin flip by construction — so a high hit rate mostly measures how much you padded.

It shows up in playtesting: one tester called 11 out of 11 correctly on their worst-played night, and 6 out of 11 on their best.

So the card reports where she surprised you rather than how often you were right. The surprises are the rounds you learned something from.

If she keeps sending back perfectly good drinks, nothing is broken. Order something you expect her to refuse.

P. C. Wason, “On the failure to eliminate hypotheses in a conceptual task”, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1960.
