2, 4, 6

2, 4, 6

In 1960 Peter Wason put those three numbers in front of people and asked them to work out the rule behind them. They could test as many sequences as they liked. He answered yes or no, and nothing else.

Most of them saw it straight away — numbers going up in twos — and set about checking. 8, 10, 12: yes. 20, 22, 24: yes. Every test came back yes. They got surer with each one. Then they declared, and they were wrong.

The rule was any three increasing numbers. It had been in plain sight from the start. They missed it because they only ever tried sequences they already expected him to accept, and a yes to a question you know the answer to is worth nothing.

Why the end screen counts surprises

Same trap, different side of the bar. Serve what you are sure they'll take and their taking it teaches you nothing. Serve the round that would actually settle which of two live theories is right, and you have set yourself something close to a coin flip — that is what settling it means. So calling every round correctly is rarely a sign of a good night. It is usually a sign you kept pouring safe.

It showed up in playtesting. One tester called 11 out of 11 on the night they played worst. On the night they played best, they called 6.

So the card counts where they caught you out rather than how often you were right. Those are the rounds you learned something in, and padding cannot buy you a single one.

The notebook, open or shut

Every usual they could be running is written out on one page, grouped, in plain sentences — and theirs is on it. That is a promise, and the game is built to keep it. Each round you serve strikes off whatever it kills. It lies open unless you shut it.

Open, you are crossing off someone else's list. It is a proper way to play, it is meant to be comfortable, and you will still be wrong more often than you expect.

Shut, the page is empty. Before you can test a theory you have to think of it, and that is the task Wason set: not picking the sentence, but writing it. The card notes when you played that way.

Either way, if they keep sliding back perfectly good drinks, nothing is broken and you are not doing badly. They are waiting to be asked something you don't already know the answer to. Serve the round you expect them to refuse.

P. C. Wason, “On the failure to eliminate hypotheses in a conceptual task”, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1960.
